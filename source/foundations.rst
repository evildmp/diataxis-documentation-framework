Foundations
===========

..  sidebar::

    You don't need to read a single word of this page to use Diátaxis effectively or even to understand it properly.

    The only reason for reading it is to have a better appreciation of some of the thinking and theorising behind Diátaxis – *why* it works, not how it works.

Diátaxis is successful because it *works* – it gives both users and creators a better experience of documentation. It makes sense and it feels right.

However, that's not enough to be confident in Diátaxis as a theory of documentation. As a theory, it needs to show why it works. It needs to show that there is actually some reason why there are exactly four kinds of documentation for example, and not three or five. It needs to demonstrate rigorous thinking and analysis, and that it stands on a sound theoretical foundation.

Otherwise, it will be just another useful heuristic approach, and the strongest claim we can make for it is that "it seems to work quite well".


The underlying principle
------------------------

Diátaxis is based on an understanding of the nature of the structure of skill. That allows us to understand something about the practitioner of a skill, and then have something to say about what guidance is appropriate to their needs.

..  sidebar::

    On this page I will refer to "guidance", rather than to "documentation". Documentation is guidance in written form, and what I have to say here applies to all guidance for skill.

The key claim of Diátaxis is that skill has certain characteristics, that we can lay out structurally.

Skill contains particular structures and relationships. At any given time, where a practitioner of skill finds themselves within those structures and relationships points at the corresponding characteristics appropriate and effective guidance will have.

Skill and guidance can be overlaid upon each other to the extent that the same structure is able to describe both. This page shows how we can work from an understanding of skill to the characteristics that documentation must have, and why there are four distinct kinds, and why they exist in a particular relationship to each other.


The nature of craft
-------------------

Let's start with *craft*. (A craft defines a domain of skill, and for most practical purposes in this discussion we can use words like *practice*, *craft* and *skill* fairly interchangeably.)

The use of a tool or product is a craft. So is an entire discipline or profession. Using a programming language is a craft, and so is flying a particular aircraft, or simply being a pilot in general.


Practical expression and conceptual grasp
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

..  diataxis-diagram:: x-axis
    :title: Two halves of the map, practical expression and conceptual grasp
    :desc:
      Above: practical expression, correct action, execution, doing
      Below: truth, knowing, conception

    :top-half: manual, correct action, **practical expression**, execution, doing
    :bottom-half: truth, knowing, **conceptual grasp**, conception, mental

Any skill (or craft or practice) contains both **practical expression** and **conceptual grasp**.

Skill contains both, and they are enmeshed in each other, but at any given moment, one of these is the key concern.

Sometimes what matters is what the pilot *does* (practices or executes an operation) and sometimes it's what the pilot *knows* (the correct heading, what a certain cloud formation indicates). In the fluid realities of any skill – and aviation is no different – the two conditions ceaselessly succeed each other, but they remain clearly distinct: sometimes what matters in skill belongs to the upper half of the diagram, and sometimes to the lower.

It doesn't matter very much how we express the distinction between the two domains; there are multiple pairs of ideas that can do the work. Different ways of articulating it might fit better with a particular case.


Development and application of skill
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

..  diataxis-diagram:: y-axis
    :title: Two halves of the map, development and application of skill
    :desc:
      Left: developing skill, becoming good at, being at study
      Right: applying skill, being good at, being at work

    :left-half: being at study, acquiring potential, **development of skill**, becoming good at, practitioner focus
    :right-half: being at work, exercising potential, **application of skill**, being good at, results-focus

Skill is something someone has. A practitioner stands in relation to their practice.

We acquire and use skill. A practice is something that needs to be both **developed**, and **applied**. Sometimes we are "at work" and sometimes we are "at study", and so on.

Once again these are two distinct conditions of the practitioner. In any kind of reality, they are bound up with each other, but that doesn't mean that we can't identify them clearly.

For example, a student pilot who is learning to fly is indeed also actually flying the plane, applying the skills they have acquired so far. But what matters, what we really care about at these times, is the *development* of their skill. When a pilot is performing their job, what matters is the correct, successful *application* of their skill. That's what the point of that activity is (even though it would be a disappointing pilot, however experienced, who failed to develop their skills through their flying).

As for the other axis, we can quickly identify multiple counterparts that represent this relationship. The particular choice of pair or form of words matters much less than the distinction that they maintain.


The map of the territory
------------------------

The distinctions and the counterparts that describe them give us two axes, on which we can lay out a map of the territory of craft.

I've chosen labels that I hope have the most general application, but it should be possible to substitute any of the counterparts suggested above, or find others, and make as much sense.

..  diataxis-diagram::  axes
    :title: The territory of craft as a two-dimensional map
    :desc: The map is defined by two axes that divide it into four quadrants.
      Above and below, practical expression versus conceptual grasp.
      Left and right, the development of skill versus application of skill.

    :axis-label-top:       Practical expression
    :axis-label-bottom:    Conceptual grasp
    :axis-label-left:      Development of skill
    :axis-label-right:     Application of skill



The vertical axis concerns the nature of skill itself. The horizontal axis concerns our relation to skill.

It's a *complete* map. There are only two dimensions, and they don't just cover the entire territory, they define it. This is why there are necessarily four quarters to it, and there could not be three, or five. It is not an arbitrary number.


What the map can tell us
-------------------------

If these axes represent a good map of the territory of craft, then what can we say about the guidance or documentation that is appropriate to what we find in it?

In reality, there is little that we can read *directly* from the map. For the horizontal axis, we can say for example that *development of skill* demands guidance that *serves the development of skill*. Which is true, but it's not exactly informative.

..  sidebar::

    It's not even the kind of thing we should *want* to read off a map.

We get a little further with the vertical axis. We can sense strongly and clearly that guidance that *directs action* is appropriate to *doing*, and that guidance that *describes what is the case* is appropriate to thinking. We can see immediately what *form* they must have to serve the corresponding axes of skill – so that's a little more than we get for the other axis, but it's still not very much.

**We shouldn't expect more.** That's not the point of the map at all. To discover and understand what forms of guidance or documentation are appropriate and effective is work we have to do, based on our understanding of human beings. We'll never close the book on the subject.

What the map does point out is how the practitioner of skill finds themselves in four fundamentally distinct conditions, in the four quadrants described by the two axes. And, they are not just four distinct conditions: they have a structural relationship to each other. This is indeed new, interesting and informative.

In turn, even though we can't directly discover precisely what the guidance that fits must do, we can see the combination of characteristics that belongs to each quadrant. We can see that the quadrants indicate four fundamentally different kinds of guidance to address these conditions of the practitioner. And, we can see how the structural relationships that hold for skill also hold for guidance, and that they map on to each other.


Documentation that answers to needs
-----------------------------------

The map of the territory of craft is what gives us the familiar Diátaxis map of documentation. We can see what kind of guidance belongs in each quadrant, and from that we can identify clearly the documentation that can provide it.

What belongs is what fits, what aligns with the characteristics of skill.

Working with clusters of meaning
--------------------------------

In most languages, words tend to fall quite awkwardly across some of the boundaries we are trying to work with.

For example, Diátaxis refers to "practical expression" as an aspect of skill that's opposed to "conceptual grasp". But theory and concept are *part of* practice, not opposed to it. Their counterpart is *action*, and the truth is that if it were not so hideous, "actional expression" would be more accurate than "practical expression".

But rather than reaching for strained and ugly ways of speaking to avoid natural language's ambiguities and shades of meaning, it's better to allow clusters of meaning and association to do the work.

That's why we use so many different words, metaphors and images in Diátaxis to express this rather small collection of ideas. They form *clusters of meaning*. It's not just more accessible to the intuition and imagination than any pinned-down set of rigidly precise terms could ever be, but also genuinely more useful.

*Practical expression* versus *conceptual grasp*, *action* versus *theory*, *doing* versus *thinking* – they're not precisely the same pairs, but they place us in the same territory; similarly, *development* versus *application* of skill, *being at "study"* rather than *being at work* and so on.

We need to be able to reach for multiple different ways of articulating these meanings, because different ones will be right for the case or perspective we're speaking of.

There might be shades of meaning around the clusters and the words in them, but their centre of gravity is clear. People are intelligent and imaginative. They will find their own connections and anchors in them, and that is much more valuable than having precise terms for every idea.

With that in mind, you should find your own connections and related meaning in these diagrams.
